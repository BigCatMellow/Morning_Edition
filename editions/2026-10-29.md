# Morning Edition — October 29, 2026

Spain's rail disaster; Haiti's deepening hunger; a disputed Sudan market strike; and Costa Rica's trade reversal.

## Lead Story

### At least 68 killed in Spain's second deadly high-speed rail disaster this year
Two trains on the Madrid–Seville line collided near Urda, Toledo, on October 28 after a signalling failure and a disputed decision to resume traffic. Regional authorities reported at least 68 deaths. Investigators are examining the control-room instructions; no final cause has been established.

**Why it matters:** The second major Spanish high-speed rail tragedy in 2026 raises urgent questions about how automated safety systems, manual overrides and railway oversight interact.

*El País — Liliana Ochoa and Spanish rail reporting — 2026-10-28*

## Front Page

### New assessment finds 5.94 million people in Haiti face acute food insecurity
A UN-backed food-security assessment estimates 5.94 million Haitians in crisis-level hunger or worse for September 2026–February 2027, including 1.8 million in the emergency category. Gang violence, displacement, high prices and rainfall deficits compound one another; the outlook for spring 2027 is worse.

**Why it matters:** More than half the assessed population lacks dependable access to sufficient food. The problem is not only food supply but insecurity that prevents farming, transport and humanitarian delivery.

*Associated Press — Haiti food-security reporting — 2026-10-29*

### Nyala market strike kills civilians as competing Sudan death tolls remain unresolved
A drone strike hit the Tangoj night market in Nyala, South Darfur, on October 26. Sudanese medical sources report at least 21 deaths; a rights group alleges more than 100, while Médecins Sans Frontières says 36 bodies reached a supported hospital and more than 150 injured people were treated. These counts are not interchangeable or independently settled.

**Why it matters:** An attack where civilians were trading near fighters illustrates the growing danger of drone warfare in Sudan and the difficulty of establishing reliable casualty counts amid restricted access.

*Sudan Tribune — reporting from Nyala — 2026-10-28*

### Costa Rica abandons Pacific trade pact bid and removes trade minister
President Laura Fernández ordered Costa Rica to stop its accession process to the 12-member CPTPP and dismissed trade minister Indiana Trejos on October 28. She argued the negotiated terms had not been adequately shared with affected sectors; agricultural producer groups had protested the deal.

**Why it matters:** The reversal exposes the domestic political trade-offs behind global trade integration, particularly when export opportunities collide with concerns about farmers and transparency.

*La Nación — Óscar Rodríguez — 2026-10-28*

## In Case You Missed It

### Recent miss: US and Japan announce plans for joint missile production in Japan
Japan's defense minister Shinjiro Koizumi announced a plan for a Japanese facility to jointly produce advanced air-to-air missiles with the United States after talks in Cebu alongside regional defense meetings. The announcement describes a planned production capability, not a completed factory.

**Why it matters:** The proposal would deepen industrial cooperation between two treaty allies and reflects how Indo-Pacific deterrence increasingly depends on ammunition supply and manufacturing capacity.

*Associated Press — regional defense reporting — 2026-10-29*

### Older catch-up: simple phone tutoring protected learning across five countries
Five randomized trials published September 9 found that short, targeted phone tutorials improved primary-school numeracy during school disruptions in India, Kenya, Nepal, the Philippines and Uganda. Text messages alone produced mixed results; phone-based tutoring worked under both NGO and government delivery models.

**Why it matters:** The durable lesson is that effective teaching design can matter more than sophisticated technology, particularly where internet access is limited or schools are closed.

*Nature — Angrist, Ainomugisha, Bathena and colleagues — 2026-09-09*

## Outside the Bubble

### A narwhal's spiral tusk is built from microscopic structures twisting in opposite directions
Imaging across scales reveals that the outward left-handed helix of a narwhal tusk emerges from mineralized collagen patterns: the outer cementum twists one way, while inner dentine contains an opposing arrangement. The study was published in August, not this week.

**Why it matters:** The result is an elegant example of how biological materials organize complex large-scale forms from microscopic architecture, without resolving every question about the tusk's evolutionary function.

*Nature Communications — Rodriguez-Palomo, Vibe and colleagues — 2026-08-18*

## At Human Scale

### In Johannesburg, neighbors learn to garden together at a once-neglected house
At 12 Park Lane in Parktown, sisters Molemo and Pelonomi Moiloa helped turn an overgrown property into a community garden. Monthly open days bring neighbors together to prune, plant, weed, share a harvest and catch up; the project began with a street clean-up involving about 50 people.

**Why it matters:** A small, specific view of urban community life in South Africa: shared space becomes a routine built through neighbors' work rather than a headline-making intervention.

*Johannesburg In Your Pocket — Unathi Nkhoma — 2026-10-29*

## Ideas

### Why competition in games depends on a shared agreement to cooperate
Game designer Eric Zimmerman argues that play's conflicts are unusual: rivals consent to a common framework that makes struggle meaningful. Drawing on Gregory Bateson, Jean Piaget and Bernie De Koven, he asks how rules, role-playing and shared trust allow competition to teach social cooperation.

**Why it matters:** The argument distinguishes constructive disagreement from hostility and suggests that the quality of a game depends as much on how people play together as on who wins.

## Worth Your Time

### How a 1607 flood became a warning from heaven in Britain's early news culture
Erik Crnkovich reads seventeenth-century pamphlets about the Bristol Channel flood to show how a real catastrophe became material for religious warning, political interpretation and sensational print. The essay separates the flood's history from the stories printers used to make it meaningful.

**Why it matters:** A compelling history of how people interpreted disaster before modern meteorology—and of the old problems of evidence, incentives and audience attention in a new medium.

*The Public Domain Review — Erik Crnkovich — 2026-10-28*
