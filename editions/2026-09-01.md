### Algeria’s wildfire death toll may be far above the government’s official count

Le Monde’s Algeria correspondent reports that independent counts built from municipal pages, death notices and local associations indicate more than 200 people may have died in the August 26–30 wildfires in Jijel and neighboring provinces. Algeria’s last public national toll remains 12. In Jijel alone, one municipal page listed more than 70 deaths, while an independent journalist’s running count reached at least 200 in the province.

**Why it matters:** The gap between the official and independently assembled tolls changes both the scale of the disaster and the governance story around it. Extreme heat and wind drove fires across more than 100 kilometres of coast and mountains, but the final human cost is still uncertain. The independent total is credible enough to take seriously, but it is not yet an official or fully verified national count.

### A funding crisis is forcing WFP to halve food assistance in the West Bank

The World Food Programme says it will cut the number of people receiving food assistance in the West Bank from 400,000 to 200,000 this month because of a funding shortfall. WFP estimates 900,000 people there are food insecure, while 76% of households have suffered a significant income decline; funding pressure is also reducing cash support in Gaza.

**Why it matters:** This is a humanitarian deterioration caused not by a new battlefield event but by the financing system behind relief. WFP says food needs in the West Bank have more than doubled since 2023 and it needs an additional $386 million over the next six months to serve an estimated two million food-insecure people across Gaza and the West Bank.

**Why it matters:** The question is no longer who was declared the winner; it is whether Zambia’s institutions can contain political conflict without criminalizing opposition or weakening judicial review. ### India’s growth engine is broadening as private investment finally accelerates

India’s economy grew 7.8% year over year in the April–June quarter, while private investment rose 11.9% and gross fixed capital formation increased to 34.3% of GDP from 31.4% a year earlier. Bank credit is expanding at its fastest pace in about a decade, with investment flowing into manufacturing, data centres, semiconductors, defence and infrastructure-linked sectors.

**Why it matters:** For years India’s growth story depended heavily on public capital spending and consumption while private companies remained cautious. A sustained private-investment cycle would make growth broader and potentially more durable. The caveat is employment: increasingly capital-intensive projects can lift output without creating jobs at the same rate.

### Brazil’s economy slowed as high interest rates finally reached household spending

Brazil’s GDP grew 0.5% from the first quarter to the second, down from 1.1% growth in the previous period. Household consumption fell 0.4%, while productive investment still rose 1.2%. The central bank has begun cutting rates, but the Selic remains 14% after peaking at 15%.

**Why it matters:** Brazil is showing the delayed trade-off of using very high interest rates to control inflation. The labor market remains comparatively strong, but household debt and expensive credit are now restraining consumption. The mix matters for the election-year economy because it separates a slowing headline from the still-resilient pieces underneath it.

### Odesa is debating whether its official 1794 founding date preserves a Russian imperial myth

The command of Ukraine’s Odesa operational group has asked the city administration to reconsider September 2, 1794 as the city’s official founding date. Its letter points to the city charter’s own reference to the port settlement of Kachibey/Hadzhibey in 1415 and argues that treating the Russian imperial reorganization of 1794 as the beginning erases a much older history.

**Why it matters:** Founding dates look ceremonial until states use history to justify present-day claims. The dispute is a compact example of how war turns archives, names and municipal rituals into political terrain. The military proposal is not a historical verdict; it calls for an expert discussion before the city changes its charter.

### Colombia has moved from rescue toward reconstruction after an earthquake that damaged more than 230,000 homes

Three weeks after the August 10 magnitude-7.4 earthquake, Colombia’s government says the official toll is 331 dead, 4,497 injured and 152 still missing. More than 452,000 people were affected across 497 municipalities, and the government has begun a reconstruction phase covering housing, schools, hospitals, roads and productive infrastructure while search operations continue.

**Why it matters:** The earthquake was large enough to become a national reconstruction problem, yet it never appeared in recent Morning Editions. The shift from emergency response to rebuilding makes this a useful moment to catch up: the challenge is now not only finding the missing but tracking how a multibillion-dollar recovery is allocated across hundreds of municipalities.

### Books and screens

University librarian Carlo Iacono argues that the decline in sustained reading is often misdiagnosed as a simple battle between books and screens. His more useful distinction is between environments designed for attention and environments designed to fragment it: a screen can host either a serious archive or an infinite feed.

**Why it matters:** The essay reframes literacy as the ability to build conditions in which understanding can happen across text, audio, video and other forms. You do not have to accept every optimistic claim about multimodal learning for the central argument to be valuable: if distraction is partly designed, then it is not an inevitable property of digital media.
