Alternative for Germany won 39 of 83 seats in Saxony-Anhalt’s state election with nearly 44% of the vote, more than doubling its 2021 share and finishing far ahead of Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s CDU. The result is historic for the far right in postwar Germany, but it is not an outright majority. ### Jamaica is asking King Charles to send slavery-reparations questions to the Privy Council

Jamaica has formally petitioned King Charles III to refer legal questions about Britain’s role in slavery and reparative justice to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council. The petition asks, among other things, whether slavery violated English common law and whether Britain owes reparative justice to Jamaicans. The king acts on government advice, and the British government continues to reject reparations.

**Why it matters:** This shifts part of the Caribbean reparations campaign from diplomacy and historical argument into an unusual constitutional and legal channel. A referral would not automatically produce compensation, and the petition may go nowhere, but it could force a court closely tied to Britain’s imperial legal inheritance to address questions that governments have largely kept political.

### China is turning a voluntary 60-day promise to auto suppliers into a more enforceable payment regime

Chinese regulators issued stricter rules governing how automakers pay suppliers, clarifying when the payment clock starts, discouraging non-cash instruments that can delay usable payment, widening coverage beyond parts suppliers, and requiring regular reporting. The rules follow a 2025 industry pledge by major automakers to pay suppliers within 60 days after complaints that price competition was pushing financial strain down the supply chain.

**Why it matters:** China’s electric-vehicle price war is often measured through sticker prices and factory output, but supplier credit is another way automakers can finance competition. Tightening payment rules shifts some of that burden back toward large manufacturers and could matter for thousands of smaller firms whose cash flow is vulnerable to delayed settlement. Enforcement, not the written limit alone, will determine the effect.

### Kenya’s foreign-hawker crackdown is becoming a test of how far investor openness extends into local retail

Kenya’s trade ministry says visa-free entry does not authorize employment or retail trade and warned that foreigners who violate work-permit rules can lose their immigration status. The clarification follows President William Ruto’s order for a crackdown on foreign nationals operating small-scale hawking and retail businesses. Other officials have stressed that properly documented and licensed foreign traders remain protected, making the policy narrower—and less settled—than a blanket ban on foreign business.

**Why it matters:** Kenya has promoted itself as open to regional mobility and foreign investment, while small traders face intense competition and political pressure at home. The dispute exposes where governments draw the line between welcoming capital and reserving parts of the informal economy for citizens. The legal scope matters because Kenya also has obligations under East African regional-mobility rules.

### Hubble has found a changing ten-sided atmospheric wave around Saturn’s south pole

Hubble observations have revealed a large roughly decagonal wave encircling Saturn’s south polar region. Researchers reconstructed its evolution across observations beginning in 2023 and report it as the first large regular-sided jet pattern identified around the planet’s south pole, distinct from Saturn’s famous north-polar hexagon.

**Why it matters:** Planetary atmospheres can spontaneously organize into long-lived geometric waves, providing natural laboratories for fluid dynamics that cannot be reproduced at planetary scale on Earth. The observation does not yet establish why the southern feature has ten sides or how stable it will be; its changing shape is part of what makes continued observation useful.

## Ideas

### Nietzsche’s genealogy can be read as a method for asking what moral shame is doing to us

Avery Snelson’s review of Allison Merrick’s *Nietzsche on the Methods and Aims of Philosophy* examines an interpretation of Nietzschean genealogy as functional analysis: moral systems do not merely state values but can help people manage feelings of powerlessness by translating suffering into guilt, shame and a sense that one’s condition is somehow deserved or controllable. Snelson finds the treatment of shame persuasive while challenging parts of Merrick’s account of Nietzsche’s psychology and the role of religion.

**Why it matters:** The useful question is methodological rather than whether Nietzsche was simply ‘against morality.’ If a moral belief persists partly because of the psychological work it performs, criticizing its truth or consistency may miss why people need it. Genealogy asks where a value came from, what emotional problem it solves, whom it serves and what kind of person it produces.

[Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/nietzsche-on-the-methods-and-aims-of-philosophy/)

## Worth Your Time

### No one is self-made: Zhuangzi’s case against meritocracy

Philosopher Christine Abigail L. Tan uses Zhuangzi to attack the moral grammar behind meritocracy: the assumption that individuals author their achievements sufficiently on their own to deserve the social rank and rewards that follow. Zhuangzi’s alternative treats action as co-produced by upbringing, institutions, luck, relationships and material conditions—the ‘wind’ that makes apparent individual flight possible.

**Why it’s worth your time:** The essay goes deeper than the familiar claim that opportunity is unequal. It asks whether desert itself is overstated whenever success is inseparable from conditions nobody created alone. You need not accept the essay’s strongest anti-meritocratic conclusion for its central challenge to remain useful: how much of an outcome can a person morally own when agency is always socially scaffolded?
