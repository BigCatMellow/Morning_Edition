# Morning Edition — October 31, 2026

A UN mission's uncertain future, contested maritime incidents, a Polish judicial crisis, and a quieter village Samhain.

## Lead Story

### Rival UN resolutions fail, leaving Western Sahara peacekeeping mandate to expire
Russia vetoed a US proposal that would have favored Moroccan sovereignty and changed the UN mission in Western Sahara. A Russian proposal to extend the existing mandate then failed to win enough votes. The 35-year-old MINURSO mission's mandate expires October 31, with the territory's political status still unresolved.

**Why it matters:** A monitoring mission may end without a replacement agreement. Morocco, the Polisario Front and outside powers disagree over whether autonomy under Moroccan sovereignty or a vote on independence should guide the settlement.

*Associated Press — Edith M. Lederer — 2026-10-30*

## Front Page

### Iran claims four tankers hit mines; maritime monitors confirm separate incidents
Iran's Revolutionary Guards said four tankers struck mines and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz on October 30. Reuters and AP could not independently verify that tally or identify all four ships. Oman reported evacuating 25 crew from a damaged tanker, while UK maritime monitors recorded projectile strikes whose relationship to Iran's claims is unclear.

**Why it matters:** The difference between a belligerent's claims and independently documented shipping incidents matters in a waterway central to global energy trade.

*Reuters — Middle East maritime reporting — 2026-10-30*

### Poland's constitutional court standoff brings president back from flight to Senegal
A judge announced the suspension of Poland's Constitutional Tribunal chief on October 30 amid a long-running dispute over appointments. Police secured the court as protesters gathered. President Karol Nawrocki turned his plane around, while government and opposition figures offered sharply different accounts of the legality of the intervention.

**Why it matters:** A contest over who may lawfully lead the court tests the credibility of attempts to repair judicial independence without reproducing political control.

*Rzeczpospolita — Polish live legal reporting — 2026-10-30*

### Conflicting reports of deadly drone strikes on Ethiopian displacement camp
Independent reporting describes drone strikes on the Jari 2 camp in Ethiopia's Amhara region on October 29. Reuters reported at least 15 deaths from rescue sources; Anadolu reported at least 16 and Ethiopian outlet Borkena cited local estimates ranging higher. Neither the responsible force nor a definitive toll has been independently established.

**Why it matters:** Displaced families have again been exposed to violence, while inconsistent casualty accounts and uncertain attribution demand careful verification rather than premature certainty.

*Borkena — Ethiopian community-source reporting — 2026-10-30*

## In Case You Missed It

### Recent miss: Oleshky evacuation convoys begin under a rare local truce
Buses began taking civilians out of Russian-occupied Oleshky on October 30 under a six-day localized pause. Ukrainian and Russian ombuds officials participated; residents were offered choices to leave for either side or stay with replenished aid. Numbers evacuated and whether the model can be repeated remain unsettled.

**Why it matters:** A limited, observable humanitarian opening is different from a general ceasefire, but may show what narrowly negotiated civilian protections can achieve.

*Associated Press — Hanna Arhirova, Kherson field reporting — 2026-10-30*

### Older catch-up: prime assembly broadens the range of possible genome edits
A September 16 Nature paper describes 'prime assembly,' a CRISPR-guided method for integrating larger DNA fragments and making rearrangements in human cells. The team demonstrated activity in primary human cells and non-dividing cells; this is laboratory method development, not a clinical treatment.

**Why it matters:** The technique addresses a practical limitation of gene editing: changing more than a few DNA letters without relying on conventional double-strand-break approaches.

*Nature — prime assembly research team — 2026-09-16*

## Outside the Bubble

### To decipher burned Roman scrolls, researchers made and burned new ones
Researchers reproduced charred papyrus and tested lead-containing inks with X-ray imaging. A September PLOS One study found that even small amounts of lead made letters much easier to detect in their model scrolls. Whether surviving Herculaneum scrolls contain enough leaded ink is still unknown.

**Why it matters:** An inventive experiment may help scholars decide which fragile ancient texts can be scanned without opening them.

*UC Berkeley / Phys.org — Robert Sanders — 2026-09-18*

## At Human Scale

### A village in León revives Samhain with chestnuts, storytelling and folk dance
Rioscuro de Laciana, in Spain's León province, has scheduled an October 31 Samhain gathering with a walk through the village, children's activities, a shared intergenerational 'calecho,' roasted chestnuts and traditional dance. Local reporting presents it as a community celebration of seasonal memory.

**Why it matters:** A small village's autumn calendar offers a view of ordinary cultural life beyond the scale of national headlines.

*iLeón — local Laciana festival reporting — 2026-10-30*

## Ideas

### If a species can be recreated, what exactly has been saved?
Historian Sadiah Qureshi argues that frozen genomes and engineered substitutes should not be mistaken for preserving a living species, its learned behavior and ecological relationships. Her essay asks whether promises of de-extinction could weaken our obligations to protect existing habitats and animals.

**Why it matters:** The question changes how conservation success is defined: genetic similarity, ecological function and a living way of being are not interchangeable.

*Aeon — Sadiah Qureshi — 2026-03-12*

## Worth Your Time

### A scientist's case for optimism begins with uncertainty, not certainty
In Undark, biologist C. Brandon Ogbunu reviews Stuart Firestein's It Could Be Otherwise, a book arguing that scientific uncertainty and historical contingency are reasons to remain curious, not reasons to despair. The review also distinguishes that intellectual optimism from empty assurances that progress is inevitable.

**Why it matters:** A reflective account of how science advances through incomplete knowledge offers a counterweight to both technological triumphalism and fatalism.
