# Morning Edition — August 25, 2026

Iran's economic isolation campaign widens, Ukraine puts drones at the center of modern warfare, and today's edition adds missed stories, evergreen ideas, and one deliberately small piece of local joy.

## The Big Story

### Washington is trying to make doing business with Iran economically dangerous

The United States has launched an expanded sanctions campaign called Operation Economic Outcast, threatening secondary sanctions against countries, banks, shipping firms and other entities that maintain key economic ties with Iran. ABC Australia reports that the first phase leans heavily on pressure and warnings, while leaving harsher enforcement measures in reserve.

**Why it matters:** This is not only about Iran. It tests how much geopolitical power the United States can still derive from the dollar, access to U.S. markets and the global financial system—and how strongly countries such as China will resist when that leverage is used against them.

## World

### Ukraine's Independence Day parade replaced marching soldiers with robots

Ukraine staged what it described as its first aerial, ground and naval drone parade, displaying unmanned ground vehicles, drone boats and aerial systems rather than traditional formations of troops and tanks. ABC reports that Ukraine says its ground robots have already carried out more than 66,000 missions this year.

**Why it matters:** The spectacle was propaganda, but it also made a serious point: autonomous and remotely operated systems are no longer a speculative future of warfare. Ukraine is building military institutions, production capacity and battlefield doctrine around them now.

### Forecasters warn the developing El Niño could become the strongest on record

Reuters reports that major forecasting centers expect Pacific temperatures to rise sharply as the 2026 El Niño strengthens, with the UK Met Office projecting conditions that could surpass previous major events. Its effects are already entangled with floods, heat, drought and agricultural stress across several regions.

**Why it matters:** A very strong El Niño can turn climate change from a slow background pressure into a near-term global economic event by affecting food production, water supplies, insurance losses, wildfire risk and energy demand at the same time.

Gao Zhen, a 70-year-old artist known for politically provocative works about Mao Zedong and the Cultural Revolution, has been sentenced to three years in prison under China's law against slandering national heroes and martyrs. The works at issue predated the law by years.

**Why it matters:** The case is about more than censorship of art. Control over historical memory determines which interpretations of a country's past can be used to criticize its present.

ABC's Ian Verrender connects expensive fuel and fertiliser, disrupted grain routes, drought, war and El Niño into a single risk picture for global food supply. The pressure is especially dangerous for import-dependent countries with weak currencies or high debt.

**Why it matters:** Food inflation is one of those problems that rarely stays in its lane. It can feed migration, unrest, debt crises, malnutrition and political instability long after the original weather or shipping shock has passed.

## Science & Technology

### Doctors injected a patient's own mitochondria into her eyes

Researchers extracted mitochondria from a woman's leg muscle and injected them into damaged eyes in an experimental attempt to restore retinal and optic-nerve function. Nature reports the procedure appeared safe and briefly restored some physiological response to light, but did not restore normal vision; the result remains preliminary.

**Why it matters:** The striking idea is that mitochondria—the energy-producing machinery inside cells—might eventually be treated almost like transplantable cellular components rather than fixed parts of a damaged cell.

*Nature · United Kingdom · science news · published 2026-08-24*

[Read the source](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02616-z)

### A study estimates AI-assisted writing now appears in most biomedical papers

A preprint analyzed language patterns across biomedical papers and estimated that almost nine in ten papers published in December 2025 in a major database showed signs of AI-assisted writing. Nature notes that the estimate is far higher than earlier work and that outside researchers caution the method may not represent the whole scientific literature.

**Why it matters:** Even if the exact percentage changes with better measurement, AI is becoming part of scientific authorship infrastructure. That raises harder questions about disclosure, homogenized language, hidden bias and whether generated prose can quietly reshape how fields explain themselves.

*Nature · United Kingdom · science news · published 2026-08-20*

[Read the source](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02551-z)

### Psilocybin may make the brain more responsive to context rather than simply more chaotic

New work discussed in Nature suggests psychedelics reorganize brain activity so that it becomes more aligned with a person's surroundings and experiences. That complicates the familiar idea that psychedelics work mainly by making neural activity less ordered.

**Why it matters:** It offers a plausible bridge between biology and the importance of setting in psychedelic therapy: the drug may not just alter the brain internally, but change how strongly the brain is shaped by the environment around it.

*Nature · United Kingdom · research analysis · published 2026-08-19*

## Ideas & Think Pieces

### Free will might survive determinism if we ask the right question

Albert Yuan's Noema essay argues that the important question is not whether human beings somehow escape physical causation, but whether a person can still be the meaningful author of actions produced through reflection, reasons and self-understanding. He then turns the argument toward AI agents and questions of responsibility.

**Why it matters:** It reframes an old philosophical argument in a way that becomes unexpectedly practical once software systems begin making plans and acting with apparent reasons of their own.

*Noema · United States · philosophy essay · published 2026-08-13*

[Read the source](https://www.noemamag.com/the-nature-of-free-will-in-the-age-of-ai/)

### The AI-consciousness debate may be missing the body

A Nature book review by Abeba Birhane challenges approaches to consciousness that treat it as disembodied information processing. The argument is that human minds arise through bodies embedded in physical and social worlds, not through computation in isolation.

**Why it matters:** It changes the question from 'When will a model become conscious?' to the more difficult question of what kinds of embodied relationships and lived constraints might be necessary for consciousness at all.

*Nature · United Kingdom · book review / philosophy · published 2026-08-24*

[Read the source](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02571-9)

### Arabic has more than one ordinary way to mean 'sadness'—and the distinctions matter

Writing in Psyche, Chereen Shurafa explores Arabic terms that distinguish grief oriented toward past loss, present suffocating distress, prolonged dejection and sorrow mixed with regret. She argues that language, classical philosophy and theology can preserve emotional distinctions that modern diagnostic categories flatten.

**Why it matters:** It is a reminder that psychological vocabularies are not culturally neutral. What a language makes easy to name can change how people understand suffering and what kinds of responses seem appropriate.

*Psyche · United Kingdom · psychology / philosophy essay*

[Read the source](https://psyche.co/ideas/arabic-has-four-words-for-sadness-philosophy-explains-why)

ABC reported in July that aid groups and researchers are seeing families use early marriage as a coping strategy after climate disasters and livelihood losses, particularly in places such as Bangladesh. **Why it matters:** Climate coverage often stops at temperatures, crops and infrastructure. This shows how environmental disruption can alter family decisions and life trajectories in ways that are harder to reverse.

### Australia has quietly replaced China as the Pacific's dominant infrastructure lender

The latest Lowy Institute Pacific Aid Map found Australia provided about 37% of development spending in the Pacific and has overtaken China in infrastructure lending. China's share has fallen substantially from the period when large Beijing-backed loans dominated regional debate.

**Why it matters:** The strategic competition in the Pacific is often discussed through military bases and diplomatic recognition, but roads, ports, loans and who actually delivers infrastructure may shape influence more persistently.

## Something I Didn't Know Yesterday

### 1,800 volunteers moved a 360-tonne Japanese castle more than two metres by hand

Teams in Aomori Prefecture used 30-metre ropes, rails and rollers to inch Hirosaki Castle's historic keep toward its original position. The technique, called hikiya, allows an entire structure to be moved intact rather than dismantled.

**Why it matters:** The remarkable part is not simply the weight. It is a preservation philosophy that treats a centuries-old building as movable—and turns restoration into a community event.

**Why it matters:** The useful question is larger than whether the historical analogy is exact: what happens when technological and planetary conditions change faster than inherited institutions and moral vocabularies?
