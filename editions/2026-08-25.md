# Morning Edition — August 25, 2026

A wider economic front opens around Iran, while food, climate, democratic institutions, science and the future of the post-American order compete for attention.

## The Big Story

### Washington is trying to isolate Iran from the world economy

The United States has announced an expanded pressure campaign called Operation Economic Outcast, threatening secondary sanctions against countries, banks, shipping firms and other entities that continue key economic relationships with Iran. ABC Australia reports that the first phase relies heavily on threats and diplomatic pressure, while leaving the most punishing measures in reserve.

**Why it matters:** The policy tests how much leverage the United States still gains from the central role of the dollar and U.S.-linked finance. Its effects could extend well beyond Iran by forcing major trading partners, especially China, to weigh access to U.S. markets against resistance to American sanctions.

## World

### A global food squeeze is forming at the intersection of war, fuel and climate

ABC's Ian Verrender argues that expensive diesel and fertiliser, disrupted Black Sea grain routes, drought in major producing regions and a strengthening El Niño are combining to reduce food supply and raise production costs. Australia expects its winter crop to fall substantially from last year, while Russia and Ukraine remain central to global wheat trade.

**Why it matters:** Food shocks rarely stay confined to agriculture. They can accelerate inflation, deepen poverty, increase migration pressure and destabilize governments in import-dependent countries.

### Zambia closed its top courts on the final day for an election challenge

Zambia's Constitutional Court and other top courts were closed on August 24, officially because of security concerns, just as the opposition faced its deadline to challenge the disputed August 13 presidential election. Reuters reports that opposition figures and independent observers have raised questions about the result and the treatment of challengers.

**Why it matters:** An election can be formally completed yet still lose democratic legitimacy if meaningful legal review becomes impossible. The episode is a useful case study in how institutional pressure can matter as much as ballot-day conduct.

### Japanese lawmakers are in China trying to repair a damaged political relationship

A delegation of Japanese lawmakers traveled to China for meetings with senior Communist Party officials, in the first such exchange since Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi took office. The trip is an effort to reopen political channels amid persistent security and economic tensions.

**Why it matters:** Japan and China are strategic rivals but deeply connected economies. Parliamentary diplomacy is a quieter indicator of whether governments are trying to prevent rivalry from hardening into permanent confrontation.

### The world's oceans have reached a record average surface temperature

The Guardian reports that global average sea-surface temperature reached about 21.1°C on August 24 as long-term warming combined with a developing El Niño. Hotter oceans increase marine heatwaves, add energy to storms and contribute to sea-level rise.

**Why it matters:** Ocean heat is not an isolated climate statistic. It influences rainfall, fisheries, storms, coral ecosystems and the ocean's ability to absorb carbon dioxide, making it a useful measure of stress across the climate system.

## Science & Technology

### Doctors injected a patient's own mitochondria into her eyes

Researchers extracted mitochondria from a woman's leg muscles and injected them into her eyes after severe damage to the optic nerve and retina. Nature reports that the procedure caused no obvious inflammation and briefly restored some physiological response to light, but did not restore normal vision; the result is a preprint and has not yet been peer reviewed.

**Why it matters:** Mitochondrial transplantation treats the cell's energy-producing machinery almost like a replaceable component. The early result is far from proof of a therapy, but it points toward a striking new category of regenerative medicine.

*Nature — United Kingdom · science news*

[Read the source](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02616-z)

### China's growing role in global health is becoming a governance question

In Nature, Ruby Wang argues that China's expanding health partnerships can bring lower-cost diagnostics, medicines and digital systems to lower- and middle-income countries. The open question is whether those partnerships also strengthen local capacity and protect national control over health infrastructure and data.

**Why it matters:** The geopolitics of health is moving beyond aid. Countries increasingly choose among competing technological and institutional ecosystems, and the terms of those partnerships can shape sovereignty for decades.

*Nature — United Kingdom · commentary*

[Read the source](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02622-1)

### Taiwan has charged nine people in an alleged AI-server smuggling scheme

Taiwanese authorities have indicted nine people, including employees linked to Nvidia and Supermicro, over allegations that AI servers and related technology were illegally exported to China. The case sits inside the broader effort to control access to advanced computing hardware.

**Why it matters:** AI competition increasingly depends on physical supply chains rather than software alone. Export controls only work if governments can police chips, servers, intermediaries and re-export routes.

## Ideas & Think Pieces

### Is a post-American world inevitable?

Foreign Affairs brings Kurt Campbell and Rush Doshi together with Mark Leonard to test two competing views of the international order. Campbell and Doshi argue that U.S. alliances remain more resilient than current pessimism suggests; Leonard argues that allies should accept that the United States has fundamentally changed and prepare for a more turbulent order.

**Why it matters:** This is the underlying question behind many separate headlines: are today's ruptures temporary deviations inside the postwar system, or evidence that the system itself is ending?

*Foreign Affairs — United States · analysis / discussion*

[Read the source](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/podcasts/post-american-world-inevitable)

### The AI-consciousness debate may be missing the body

A Nature book review by Abeba Birhane examines the argument that AI, psychology and philosophy often discuss consciousness as though it can be separated from embodiment. The critique is that human consciousness develops through a body acting in and being affected by a physical and social world.

**Why it matters:** It changes the question from 'When will a machine become conscious?' to 'What sort of system could have the embodied relationships from which consciousness might emerge?' That is a much harder and more philosophically useful problem.

*Nature — United Kingdom · book review / philosophy*

[Read the source](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02571-9)

### What if better sleep becomes another thing employers can measure?

Bioethicists writing in Nature examine technologies designed to enhance sleep and warn that tools intended to improve health could also turn rest into something auditable. Once sleep quality can be measured and optimized, institutions may begin treating poor sleep as an individual failure rather than a private part of life.

**Why it matters:** It is a compact example of a recurring technology problem: measurement can make something easier to improve while simultaneously making it easier for institutions to demand, score and police.

*Nature — United Kingdom · commentary / bioethics*

[Read the source](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02573-7)

## Culture & History

### Why Britain's deadliest civilian aviation accident of World War II is barely remembered

Smithsonian revisits the 1944 Freckleton air disaster, when a U.S. military test aircraft crashed into an English village school and killed 61 people, including 38 young children. The article explores both the catastrophe and the way a major wartime civilian tragedy can recede from collective memory.

**Why it matters:** History is partly shaped by what societies choose to memorialize. Events can be enormous for a community yet become almost invisible in the larger national story.

## Something I Didn't Know Yesterday

### 1,800 people helped pull a 360-tonne Japanese castle

In Aomori prefecture, about 1,800 volunteers used ropes and rollers to move the keep of Hirosaki Castle more than two metres as part of long-running restoration work. The process used hikiya, a traditional technique for moving a structure intact rather than dismantling it.

**Why it matters:** It is an unusual example of preservation engineering in which a centuries-old building is treated as something that can be carefully moved as a whole.
