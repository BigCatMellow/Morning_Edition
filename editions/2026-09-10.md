### Five randomized trials found that short, targeted phone tutoring can preserve learning when schools are disrupted

A Nature study coordinated randomized trials in India, Kenya, Nepal, the Philippines and Uganda. Weekly 20-minute phone tutorials paired with SMS produced positive learning effects in all five settings, with a pooled effect of 0.321 standard deviations; SMS alone had smaller and less consistent effects. Government teachers performed effectively in the two countries where delivery model was randomized.

**Why it matters:** This is unusually useful education evidence because the same basic intervention was tested across several countries and under government as well as NGO delivery. The finding is not that “phones solve school closure”: live targeted tutoring, repeated assessment and adaptation to each child’s level appear central, while passive messages alone were inconsistent.

## Ideas

### If desires can be right or wrong, what exactly makes a desire accurate?

Allan Hazlett’s book argues that desire has an “accuracy condition” analogous to belief: a desire is accurate when what it aims at is good and inaccurate when it aims at something bad. Alex Gregory’s review tests that proposal through Hazlett’s problem of nihilism—if nothing is good or bad, must every desire become irrational?—and argues that Hazlett’s solution creates difficult discontinuities around things of neutral value.

**Why it matters:** The argument opens a less familiar branch of epistemology. We routinely ask whether beliefs fit the truth; Hazlett asks whether wanting can also fit or fail to fit the evaluative world. That forces a deeper question about rational agency: whether reasons for wanting are merely produced by our desires, or whether desires themselves can be assessed against standards they did not create.

## Outside the Bubble

### A fluid-dynamics testbed let a reinforcement-learning controller transfer from a simple channel to a 3-D wing without retraining

HydroGym, described in Nature, provides 61 validated flow-control environments so learning algorithms can be trained on tractable fluid problems before being tested on harder ones. In a proof of concept, a controller trained on turbulent channel flow transferred without additional training to a three-dimensional wing simulation, reducing local skin-friction drag by about 38% and total drag by about 11%.

**Why it matters:** The interesting result is transfer, not a claim that an AI-designed aircraft wing is ready for service. High-fidelity fluid simulations are extremely expensive, so learning reusable control principles in simpler environments could make active flow control more practical. The authors explicitly note that their simulations remain below the Reynolds numbers typical of many industrial applications.

*Nature · 2026-08-19 · [Source](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10917-6)*

## Worth Your Time

### Humans cooperate readily person-to-person; the same mechanisms become fragile when groups face one another

Paul Van Lange and Paul Bergmann synthesize decades of research to argue that reciprocity, empathy and psychological safety make cooperation comparatively robust between individuals, while group boundaries introduce intuitive mistrust, in-group favoritism and threat dynamics that can spiral into evidence-based distrust and conflict.

**Why it matters:** The review gives a useful bridge between everyday social psychology and large political problems. It does not claim groups are inherently hostile; rather, it identifies mechanisms that make intergroup cooperation less self-sustaining and therefore more dependent on institutions, repeated contact and structures that prevent early mistrust from becoming self-confirming.
