### Bangladesh’s measles outbreak shows how quickly vaccination gaps can reverse years of disease control

**Why it matters:** Measles is extraordinarily contagious but highly preventable with vaccination. ### Periodical cicadas may still be carrying the ecological cost of forests cut nearly a century ago

An open-access *Ecology* study found that present-day periodical-cicada populations in an Iowa landscape were better predicted by 1930 tree cover than by current tree cover. Reforested sites often remained empty, consistent with the insects’ very limited dispersal and 13- or 17-year life cycles; an experimental relocation of more than 20,000 adults also failed to establish a new population.

**Why it matters:** The work shows that habitat restoration can be too slow for species whose biology depends on continuity across many decades. The authors’ extinction warning is model-based and strongest for the ecological mechanism they studied; it should not be read as proof that all periodical cicadas are imminently doomed.

### International humanitarian law may become influential in war only by becoming useful to the military institutions it is meant to restrain

In an open-access *Ethics & International Affairs* article, Beth Rowan traces how U.S. military lawyers embedded international humanitarian law inside planning, targeting and rules-of-engagement processes. Her central paradox is that law gained practical influence by being translated into forms commanders could use—but that same translation made legal restraint vulnerable to being reshaped by strategic priorities.

**Why it matters:** The argument complicates the idea that stronger law is mainly a matter of writing better treaties. Rules affect conduct when institutions make them operational. But institutional success can alter the rule’s meaning: a legal adviser who becomes indispensable may restrain violence more often while also learning to frame restraint in terms acceptable to the organization using force.

### An Arctic research program is treating adaptation as an ecosystem problem rather than a species-by-species problem

A six-year NSF-backed project in Alaska is tracking genetic and ecological change across five connected organisms—a bird, fish, willow, aquatic insect and beetle—to test whether adaptation in one species changes the adaptive possibilities of the others. The project is a research program, not evidence that whole ecosystems can successfully evolve their way through rapid warming.

**Why it matters:** Conservation often asks whether a species can tolerate a warmer climate. This project asks a harder question: whether the network of feeding, migration and nutrient relationships among species can change together quickly enough to preserve ecosystem function. The answer is unknown, which is precisely why the design is interesting.

### Climate injustice is not only losing resources; it can mean losing the stable relationships and capabilities that make a life workable

Political theorist David Schlosberg argues that climate change should be understood as ongoing “turbulence”: repeated disruption of bodies, homes, livelihoods, communities and relationships to place. Using the capabilities approach, he argues that just adaptation should protect people’s real ability to function and pursue valued lives rather than only replacing damaged assets after disasters.

**Why it matters:** The essay offers a richer account of adaptation than engineering resilience alone. A rebuilt road or compensated house may not restore a community’s health, social ties, cultural practices or sense of place. The framework is demanding and leaves measurement problems unresolved, but it makes visible losses conventional cost-benefit accounting can miss.
