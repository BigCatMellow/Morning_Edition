### An arbitration court says India cannot suspend the Indus Waters Treaty with Pakistan

The Permanent Court of Arbitration said the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty remains fully in force and ordered India to observe treaty limits while a World Bank-appointed neutral expert reviews contested hydropower designs in Kashmir. India, which suspended participation after a 2025 attack in Kashmir, rejected the ruling and says the tribunal has no jurisdiction over its sovereign decisions.

**Why it matters:** The treaty has survived wars because both countries depend on a predictable river system. The ruling therefore matters even if enforcement is contested: it sharpens the legal conflict over whether a state can suspend a long-running water treaty during a security crisis and raises the stakes around dams on rivers vital to Pakistani agriculture.

### Libya’s rival institutions signed a roadmap aimed at national elections within two years

A UN-facilitated agreement signed in Tripoli covers the first two steps of a new political roadmap: reconstituting the national election commission and resolving constitutional and legal rules for presidential and legislative elections. The parties committed to work toward elections within 24 months under a unified executive, while the House of Representatives and High State Council are supposed to validate the framework within one month.

**Why it matters:** Libya has spent years with rival governments, overlapping institutions and repeatedly delayed elections. This agreement is not a settlement by itself, but it creates specific institutional tasks, deadlines and a follow-up mechanism that can be judged against actual implementation rather than another open-ended promise of elections.

### Iceland voted 52.8% to 47.2% against reopening EU accession talks

Final results from Iceland’s referendum show a narrow national majority against restarting European Union membership negotiations. The result was sharply geographic: Reykjavík backed reopening talks while every other constituency voted no. The government has said it will respect the outcome and not restart negotiations during its current term.

**Why it matters:** The vote was not on EU membership itself, but it closes the most immediate path toward accession and shows how sovereignty, fisheries and Arctic security are pulling Iceland in different directions. Iceland will remain deeply integrated with Europe through the European Economic Area and Schengen while staying outside EU decision-making.

### Africa’s diesel supply chain is shifting from the Middle East toward Asia

Ship-tracking and trade data indicate that Asia, including India, will send roughly 1.8–2.0 million metric tons of diesel to Africa in August, the highest level in at least four and a half years. Middle Eastern shipments are estimated at only 600,000–800,000 tons, their lowest in almost nine years, as the Iran war, Red Sea risks and reduced Saudi refinery output disrupt established routes.

**Why it matters:** Fuel supply chains are adapting around conflict in real time. Africa historically sourced about half of its imported diesel from the Middle East; replacing those barrels from much farther east can keep fuel available, but it also changes freight costs, refinery incentives and exposure to Asian supply conditions. This is a concrete example of geopolitical disruption becoming ordinary economic infrastructure.

### Footprints in Kenya suggest Paranthropus boisei could be much larger—and more social—than its bones implied

A PNAS study of roughly 1.4-million-year-old trackways near Lake Turkana attributes a set of footprints to eight Paranthropus boisei individuals moving across the same lakeshore surface. Track size suggests some individuals may have stood around 1.8 metres tall and weighed about 75 kilograms, while the concentration of mostly adult-sized tracks raises the possibility of group travel.

**Why it matters:** Bones preserve anatomy; footprints can preserve behavior over a very short interval. These tracks therefore offer an unusually direct glimpse of body size and social movement in a hominin known mostly from skulls and teeth. The species assignment and social interpretation are reasoned reconstructions rather than certainties, so they should be treated as strong hypotheses rather than a photograph of a known social system.

### Africa writes back

D Vance Smith follows the history of Libyco-Berber writing across North Africa to show how European scholars repeatedly mistook missing archives for missing literacy. The essay moves from ancient inscriptions and colonial collecting to modern Amazigh identity and asks what scholars fail to see when their definitions of writing and knowledge already assume a European path of development.

**Why it matters:** It is a durable corrective to a common historical error: absence from familiar archives is not evidence that a society lacked writing, memory or intellectual life. The piece is also about method—how even sympathetic scholarship can reproduce the assumptions it intends to challenge—and pairs well with today’s deliberately broader geographic and linguistic source mix.
