# Morning Edition — October 26, 2026

Lula wins in Brazil; Serbia's students prevail; Ebola precautions widen; and cave art moves in firelight.

## Lead Story

### Lula wins a fourth term in Brazil; Bolsonaro contests the result
Brazil's electoral court declared President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva the winner of Sunday's runoff against Senator Flávio Bolsonaro. With the count complete, Lula took about 51.8% of valid votes to Bolsonaro's 48.2%, a margin of roughly 4.4 million votes. Bolsonaro refused to concede and demanded annulment after voting hours were extended in some places.

**Why it matters:** The vote settles the count, not the political conflict. Lula must govern a divided country and work with a conservative Congress, while any challenge to the result must meet a legal standard rather than substitute allegation for evidence.

## Front Page

### Serbia's student-led opposition wins the parliamentary vote
Serbia's Student List defeated the long-dominant governing camp in Sunday's snap parliamentary election, and Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat. Local election monitors projected about 140 of 250 seats for the students; final official seat allocation and the transfer of power still require confirmation.

**Why it matters:** A movement that began after the deadly 2024 Novi Sad station collapse has turned sustained street protests into an electoral majority. Governing and institutional reform will be harder than winning the vote.

*N1 Belgrade — election-night local reporting — 2026-10-25*

### Uganda orders 21-day quarantine as the Ebola outbreak crosses borders
Uganda announced a 21-day quarantine for returning citizens with recent travel in Congo as the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak spreads beyond the Democratic Republic of the Congo. WHO counted 9,704 confirmed cases and 4,687 deaths in Congo as of October 20; imported cases have been reported in Kenya and Nigeria. The route of infection for Nigeria's patient remains disputed.

**Why it matters:** Containing a regional outbreak depends on contact tracing, safe clinical care and trust. The uncertainty around one patient's itinerary is a reason to investigate, not to declare community spread in Uganda.

*Daily Monitor — Uganda national reporting — 2026-10-25*

### Houthis claim attacks on Saudi oil route; damage remains unconfirmed
Yemen's Houthi movement said Monday it targeted pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline and Riyadh airport. Saudi authorities had not confirmed damage to the pipeline or airport. The Saudi-led coalition separately reported intercepting a missile aimed toward Riyadh and striking launch sites in Yemen.

**Why it matters:** The pipeline is especially important while shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is disrupted. A claim of an attack is not evidence that export infrastructure was hit or oil flow interrupted.

*Reuters — October 26 regional conflict report — 2026-10-26*

## In Case You Missed It

### Recent miss: María Corina Machado returns to Venezuela
Venezuelan opposition leader María Corina Machado returned on October 25 after months abroad. Supporters gathered along her route to Caracas and called for elections; U.S. officials said American-backed talks helped secure her return. No firm presidential-election timetable has been established.

**Why it matters:** Her return changes the opposition's ability to organize publicly, but the terms of a transition remain contested. Crowds and diplomatic statements should not be mistaken for an agreed election date.

*El Pitazo — Kemberlyn Talero, on-the-ground reporting — 2026-10-25*

### Older catch-up: ancient cacao DNA reveals long-distance links to Etowah
Researchers found degraded cacao DNA in two pottery fragments from 11th- and 12th-century contexts at the Indigenous city of Etowah in present-day Georgia. The August 26 peer-reviewed study provides evidence of cacao at a Mississippian site far from where the crop grew; it does not establish the trade route or precisely what the vessels held.

**Why it matters:** The discovery widens the map of exchange in precolonial North America without implying that Mississippian mound builders borrowed their architecture from elsewhere.

*PLOS One — King, Powis and colleagues — 2026-08-26*

## Outside the Bubble

### Aldabra's green turtles show what long-term conservation can recover
Decades of protection have helped green turtles rebound around the Seychelles' Aldabra Atoll. A global reassessment moved the species to 'Least Concern' in 2025, but local populations remain uneven and threats such as fishing bycatch and habitat change persist.

**Why it matters:** Conservation can produce measurable recovery, yet a global category can hide places where animals still need protection. The story is both a success and a warning against treating success as completion.

*Smithsonian Magazine — Kevin Gepford — 2026-10-21*

## Ideas

### Generalisations help us think; when do they become harmful stereotypes?
Philosopher Tom Ralston argues that generalisations are habits for navigating future encounters, not merely imperfect summaries of past facts. Their usefulness does not excuse stereotyping: a sensible test asks how often a generalisation fails and who bears the cost when it does.

**Why it matters:** The argument separates the cognitive necessity of shortcuts from their moral consequences, offering a more exact way to challenge prejudice than declaring all generalisation irrational.

*Aeon — Tom Ralston — 2026-08-10*

## Worth Your Time

### Cave paintings may have been experiences, not static pictures
In northern Spain's El Castillo cave, archaeologists examine how prehistoric artists placed animals and hand stencils around natural rock shapes. Torchlight, shadows, touch and movement may have made the art seem to emerge from the cave itself, though some dramatic effects remain hypotheses.

**Why it matters:** The essay changes the question from what an image represents to how an ancient viewer encountered it, while resisting the temptation to treat an evocative reconstruction as proven intention.

*Smithsonian Magazine — Ben Crair — 2026-09-01*
