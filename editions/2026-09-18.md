### Yemen’s renewed west-coast fighting has displaced more than 100,000 people and pushed thousands across the sea to Djibouti

IOM says displacement from the escalation that began in June has climbed above 100,000, concentrated in Taiz and Lahj, while thousands of Yemenis have crossed the Gulf of Aden toward Djibouti. The latest Reuters count is about 112,000 internally displaced and nearly 3,000 who have fled by sea.

### At least 33 suspected illegal miners died in Nigerian paramilitary custody, and the cause remains disputed

Detainees arrested during illegal-mining raids in Niger State were found dead in custody of the Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps. The agency first referred to a suspected disease outbreak, later said the cause had not been established, and the interior minister suspended the state commandant while an investigation proceeds.

**Why it matters:** The central fact is mass death in state custody, not the mining allegation. Nigerian reporting and international coverage disagree on the exact toll—33, 37 or 40—while survivors cited by AP described suffocation. A medical cause has not yet been established, so the edition uses the lowest directly documented count and keeps competing explanations attributed.

### Japan raised its policy rate to 1.25%, the highest level in 31 years

The Bank of Japan voted 7–2 to lift its short-term policy rate from 1% to about 1.25%, continuing its exit from decades of near-zero borrowing costs. Two board members dissented.

**Why it matters:** Japan was the extreme case of ultra-low interest rates. A 1.25% policy rate is still modest by many countries’ standards, but the normalization changes borrowing costs, the yen and the economics of Japan’s very large public debt. The decision was widely expected; the more important signal is how far the bank is willing to continue.

### Fewer people are reaching Europe by sea this year, but more are dying or disappearing on the routes

IOM says sea arrivals were down 39% year-on-year by mid-September, to about 60,000, while at least 2,292 people had died or gone missing, up from 1,999 over the comparable period last year. The agency cautions that the real death toll is probably higher.

**Why it matters:** A falling arrival count can look like a successful border statistic while masking a worsening risk per journey. The pattern is especially important because route changes, rescue capacity, smuggling practices and enforcement can alter danger independently of how many people attempt the crossing.

## In Case You Missed It

### Cuba’s electrical grid collapsed nationwide for the sixth time this year in early August

On August 2–3, Cuba’s national grid suffered another total disconnection and then failed again during restoration. The episode followed three nationwide failures in July and came amid severe fuel shortages, aging thermal plants and chronic maintenance problems.

**Why it matters:** A blackout is temporary; repeated whole-system collapse is an infrastructure condition. The crisis affects water pumping, refrigeration, hospitals, communications and ordinary sleep during summer heat. Cuba attributes much of the strain to U.S. sanctions and fuel restrictions; independent reporting also points to old equipment, underinvestment and maintenance failures.

## Ideas

### Political philosophy may have made the household too private to think about clearly

Sandrine Bergès argues that ancient political philosophy treated the household as a genuine community with questions of authority, dependence, work and shared goods—not merely as a private refuge outside politics. Recovering that tradition changes what counts as a political problem.

**Why it matters:** The household is where people first encounter care, dependency, unequal power and the division of labor. Treating it as conceptually separate from public life can hide political assumptions inside supposedly private arrangements. The harder question is how to recognize the household’s political structure without making every intimate relationship an arm of the state.

### How one philosopher tried to rebuild African philosophy without making Europe either its master or its enemy

An Aeon essay on Lansana Keita traces a philosophical project that is Afrocentric without treating African thought as intellectually sealed off from other traditions. Keita combines criticism of inherited colonial categories with engagement across economics, science and the Western canon.

**Why it matters:** The piece is useful because it avoids two easy stories: that philosophy is a universal European inheritance simply exported elsewhere, or that intellectual decolonization requires refusing outside traditions. Keita’s harder proposal is reconstruction—asking what philosophical institutions and arguments should serve African societies now while remaining open to criticism from anywhere.
