# Morning Edition — November 23, 2026

Climate diplomacy stalls; security and accountability in Mexico and the West Bank; an AI exam reckoning; and the uses of wonder.

## Lead Story

### COP31 ends without an overarching climate agreement
After two days of overtime in Antalya, governments failed to agree a final political text. The European Union and climate-vulnerable countries rejected language that omitted a fossil-fuel transition; oil producers opposed explicit phase-down wording. Earlier climate commitments remain in force.

**Why it matters:** The failure exposes a consensus process struggling to translate climate goals into finance and fossil-fuel policy; it does not undo the Paris Agreement.

## Front Page

### Mexico arrests fugitive cartel leader after deadly operation in Sinaloa
Mexican special forces captured Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as El Chapo Isidro, in Guasave. Officials reported four soldiers and seven alleged gunmen killed, followed by burning roadblocks and disruptions for residents.

**Why it matters:** Removing a cartel leader may disrupt trafficking, but analysts warn that succession struggles can intensify violence in communities already caught between rival factions.

*Reuters — 2026-11-21*

### Arson attacks on West Bank villages draw condemnation across Israeli politics
Palestinian residents reported that settlers burned vehicles and left racist graffiti in villages near Ramallah on Sunday. Israeli police opened an investigation, and opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot condemned the attacks; no injuries were reported in the initial accounts.

**Why it matters:** The incidents add immediate danger to Palestinian communities and sharpen debate over policing, accountability and settlement policy.

*Reuters — 2026-11-22*

## In Case You Missed It

### South Korean education minister resigns after last-minute AI-cheating rules disrupt national exam
President Lee Jae Myung accepted Choi Kyo-jin's resignation Sunday after hastily revised anti-cheating measures confused students and exam administrators. The government had tightened checks on clothing, cameras and electronic devices before the high-stakes Suneung.

**Why it matters:** The case shows how AI-enabled cheating pressures institutions to react quickly, sometimes at the expense of predictable rules for students.

*Yonhap News Agency — 2026-11-22*

### Older catch-up: possible prehistoric stone anchor recovered off Norway
Marine archaeologists found a heavy grooved stone among quarry debris at Hespriniholmen, an islet associated with greenstone extraction thousands of years ago. Researchers think it may have anchored boats, but its precise use is inferred rather than demonstrated.

**Why it matters:** The find offers a tangible clue to the maritime skill needed to exploit a remote quarry over many generations.

*Archaeology Magazine — 2026-10-13*

## Ideas

### Consciousness is real, but need not be supernatural
Philosopher Massimo Pigliucci argues against treating consciousness either as an inexplicable ghostly property or as an illusion. His alternative treats conscious experience as a genuine biological phenomenon with causal significance.

**Why it matters:** The argument asks what an adequate scientific account of experience must explain without mistaking a difficult question for a magical one.

## Something to Sit With

> A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines.

*— Ralph Waldo Emerson · Essays, First Series — Self-Reliance*

Emerson is criticizing loyalty to one's earlier statements when new experience warrants a changed judgment, not arguing that evidence or integrity are unimportant.

**A question to carry:** What opinion do you maintain chiefly because you have already defended it?

## Worth Your Time

### The intellectual value of being baffled by the past
Historian Marnie Hughes-Warrington argues that historical explanation can become too neat, flattening the unfamiliar and surprising aspects of past lives. Wonder, properly disciplined by evidence, may be a tool of understanding rather than its enemy.

**Why it matters:** An unfamiliar past is an opportunity to test the categories through which we interpret the present.

*Aeon / Marnie Hughes-Warrington — *
