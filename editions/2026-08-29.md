## Science

### [Rising CO₂ can boost wild C4 savannah grasses when water is scarce](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02480-x)
A Nature study combined a meta-analysis of 70 experiments with 32 years of field observations from 533 plots in South Africa’s Kruger National Park. It found that elevated carbon dioxide increased aboveground biomass in wild C4 grasses most clearly under water-limited conditions, challenging the simple assumption that C4 grasses respond only weakly to higher CO₂.

**Why it matters:** Savannah forecasts depend on competition among grasses, woody plants, water and fire. If rising CO₂ also fertilizes C4 grasses under drought, models that mainly emphasize CO₂-driven woody encroachment may be missing part of the system. The result is a synthesis plus long-term observational evidence, not a forecast that every savannah will become more productive.

## Society & Human Behavior

### [A Swiss experiment suggests agrivoltaics can reduce ideological opposition to solar projects](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-77141-8)
A pre-registered study of 2,145 Swiss residents tested reactions to solar projects that combine electricity generation with farming. Support crossed ideological lines more readily when projects preserved agricultural yields, fit the landscape and had local ownership.

**Why it matters:** Energy debates are often treated as fixed clashes of ideology. This experiment suggests some opposition is responsive to project design and distribution of benefits, not just partisan identity. The evidence is stronger than a simple survey because the study was pre-registered and field-embedded, but it is still one national context and should not be assumed to generalize everywhere.

### [Metal brooches accidentally preserved two layers of clothing in a 1,300-year-old Danish grave](https://archaeology.org/news/2026/08/27/1300-year-old-textile-fragments-found-in-viking-womans-grave/)
Textile fragments survived on both sides of two scoop-shaped brooches in an eighth-century burial at Skonager in western Jutland. Researchers think salts released as the metal corroded suppressed the microbes that normally destroy cloth in the region’s acidic soils, leaving rare evidence of both inner and outer garments.

**Why it matters:** Archaeology often reconstructs clothing from metal fasteners because the fabric itself has vanished. Here the fasteners became miniature preservation environments. Analysis of weave, spinning and dyes may reveal what the woman actually wore rather than forcing the burial into a generalized image of ‘Viking dress.’
