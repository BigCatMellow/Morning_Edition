## Ideas

### What if words like “sadness” and “anger” do not name hidden things inside us?

Philosopher Christopher Hoyt develops a Wittgensteinian critique of psychology: psychological language may work less like labels for private inner objects and more like tools embedded in social life. On this view, understanding another person often depends on gestures, history, context and relationships rather than discovering an unseen mental mechanism.

**Why it matters:** The argument challenges an assumption shared by everyday speech and some scientific theorizing: that every meaningful psychological concept must correspond to an internal state waiting to be identified. Its strongest vulnerability is overreach. Neuroscience can discover mechanisms relevant to emotion and behavior even if ordinary words such as “anger” do more social work than merely naming those mechanisms.

### Loneliness may be the absence of a kind of experience that only exists between people

James Laing asks what people actually want when they want a “shared life.” His answer centers on interpersonal connection: an irreducibly relational emotional experience that cannot be reduced to two individuals separately having pleasant feelings at the same time. He then uses that account to explain a distinctive harm of loneliness.

**Why it matters:** The paper offers a sharper question than whether lonely people simply lack company. If some goods exist only in genuinely shared experience, then adding social contact without connection can leave the central deprivation untouched. The difficult question is whether interpersonal connection is truly irreducible or whether psychology can explain it through individual attitudes, attention and coordinated behavior.
